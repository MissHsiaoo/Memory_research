Title: Borrowing Strength Across Stores: Transfer Learning for Choice-Based Revenue Management
Recep Yusuf Bekci, Stefanus Jasin, Joline Uichanco
Subjects: Machine Learning (stat.ML); Machine Learning (cs.LG); Optimization and Control (math.OC)

Large retail chains must tailor assortment and pricing decisions to local customer preferences, yet even retailers with extensive sales records often have limited data for estimating demand at the store or local market level, particularly in product categories with short selling windows. Local estimation (fitting a demand model for each store on its own) can therefore be unreliable, while pooling data across stores improves estimation precision at the cost of ignoring local demand heterogeneity. We study how retailers can balance these competing effects through transfer learning under a multinomial logit (MNL) demand model. We propose a shrinkage estimator that combines store-specific demand estimates with information pooled from other stores. Unlike the linear or quadratic settings underlying much of the existing theoretical literature on information transfer in operations, the nonlinear MNL likelihood does not admit a straightforward bias-variance decomposition. By exploiting its self-concordant-like properties, we establish finite-sample performance guarantees that characterize when information transfer improves demand estimation and how the appropriate degree of shrinkage depends on local sample size, feature dimensionality, and cross-store heterogeneity. We further derive regret bounds for downstream assortment and pricing decisions. Numerical experiments demonstrate the operational benefits of shrinkage across different data environments. An empirical study using data from several hundred stores of a European apparel retailer shows that shrinkage improves out-of-sample product prioritization and revenue capture relative to both local estimation and full pooling. Our findings demonstrate how retailers can leverage information across their store networks to improve revenue management decisions while preserving local demand differences.

Title: A Reverse-Gold Theorem: Positive-Data Identification by Substrate-Bounded Contrastive Learners
Rakesh Sengupta
Subjects: Machine Learning (stat.ML); Computation and Language (cs.CL); Machine Learning (cs.LG); Neurons and Cognition (q-bio.NC)

Gold's theorem shows that no learner can identify an arbitrary language in a superfinite class from positive presentations alone. We prove a positive result of a different shape. Fix an innate \emph{recursive contrast operator} $R$ mapping any string to a finite set of structurally bounded perturbations of it, and a discriminative scorer of bounded capacity trained by InfoNCE-style contrastive estimation \citep{vandenoord2018,macollins2018} on self-generated perturbations. Under a faithfulness condition relating operator, scorer, and target, the trained scorer achieves \emph{PAC conditional identification} from positive data alone: with high probability it ranks all but an $\varepsilon$-fraction of positives above every out-of-language perturbation in their own orbit, with sample complexity polynomial in the capacity, the orbit size, the score bound, and $1/\varepsilon$. The negatives are constructed by the learner, not supplied by an oracle, and no global normalizing constant over strings is ever computed. The misranking bound follows from the geometry of the contrastive loss---per-example loss floors plus a definite loss increment under misranking---yielding an $\varepsilon^{-2}$ rate, together with an explicit approximation gap that quantifies the cost of in-language (``colliding'') perturbations and vanishes for orbit-pure operators. The ranking guarantee lifts to a global membership classifier under a margin condition, and the same operator composed with the learned scorer is a normalization-free Metropolis--Hastings generator. We instantiate the hypotheses for substitutable context-free languages \citep{clarkeyraud2007} and the subregular classes \citep{heinz2010,hayashikamigaito2025}, and state an open conjecture locating the construction in the mildly context-sensitive hierarchy.

Title: Depth Separation for Neural Networks under Gaussian Measure
Cynthia Bortolotto, Francisco Ganacim, Roberto I. Oliveira
Subjects: Machine Learning (stat.ML); Machine Learning (cs.LG)

We revisit the problem of depth separation for neural networks, as studied by Eldan and Shamir, Daniely, and others. Specifically, we consider the natural setting of $L^2$ approximation of radial functions under the standard Gaussian measure in $d$ dimensions. We show that there is a radial function in $L^2$ under the standard Gaussian measure that can be approximated to a fixed sufficiently small $L^2$ error by a depth-3 ReLU neural network of polynomial size in $d$ but can only be approximated by depth-2 networks with subexponentially bounded weights if their size is at least exponential in $d$. For that, we prove a general result showing that if a radial function has substantial mass in the high frequencies of its Hermite expansion, then it cannot be well approximated by a depth-2 neural network with small width and bounded weights. All theorems, lemmas, propositions, and corollaries are verified in Lean~4.

Title: Is In-Context Reinforcement Learning A Posterior Sampler? An Empirical Study
Weiqin Chen, Xinlong Du, Gesualdo Scutari, Harsha Honnappa
Subjects: Machine Learning (stat.ML); Machine Learning (cs.LG)

In-context reinforcement learning (ICRL) exploits the impressive in-context learning ability of large language models to zero-shot generalize to new, unseen tasks at inference time without any parameter updates. Among many approaches, the Decision-Pretrained Transformer (DPT) is particularly appealing for its theoretical grounding: under idealized assumptions such as a sufficiently expressive model and infinite pretraining data, DPT can perform Bayesian posterior sampling. Whether this characterization survives in practice remains open. Therefore, we ask: \textit{is ICRL, specifically DPT and our proposed DPT variants, a Bayesian posterior sampler in practice?} To approach this question, we establish two necessary conditions that a Bayesian posterior sampler must satisfy: \textit{sufficient-statistic invariance} and the \textit{martingale property}. We then construct statistical hypothesis tests to statistically assess if practically-trained ICRL models satisfy these conditions. Applying these tests in Gaussian, Bernoulli, and linear bandit settings, we find that both conditions are consistently violated, suggesting that ICRL may not behave as Bayesian posterior samplers in practice. Beyond this negative result, our tests provide a principled toolkit for testing the Bayesian behavior of ICRL.

Title: Internal Guidance as a Sampling Method for Tilted Diffusion Models
Curtis McDonald, Peter Bartlett
Subjects: Machine Learning (stat.ML); Machine Learning (cs.LG)

Reward guidance methods sample an exponential tilt of a base density using a diffusion model trained with only samples from the base distribution. Existing methods use what we call external guidance: additive terms in the drift or multiplicative terms on the transition probabilities. We propose internal guidance which leaves the output of the base denoiser unmodified and instead changes the point at which it is evaluated. For discrete distributions embedded in the hypercube $\{-1,1\}^{d}$, the denoiser of any linearly tilted density is exactly the base denoiser evaluated at a shifted and rescaled input. In practice the base denoiser is a neural network trained on noised samples of the base distribution. The required evaluation points for sampling the tilted density would be out-of-distribution for basic noising of the base distribution. We modify the training algorithm with random diagonal rescalings of the data, so that the denoiser network learns both in- and out-of-distribution evaluations of the denoiser relative to the base distribution. A single denoiser network samples binary reward tilts, class labels, and infilling with no fine-tuning and no external guidance term.

Subjects: Machine Learning (stat.ML); Machine Learning (cs.LG)

Barycenter models, based on stochastic and deterministic optimal transport bridges between probability measures, have proven to be useful for supervised and unsupervised learning for (probability) measure-valued data. Title: When Thoughts Become Memory: Unifying In-Context Learning and Fine-Tuning
Leon Chlon, Julie Huang
Subjects: Machine Learning (stat.ML); Machine Learning (cs.LG)

We are the first to give a certified rule for whether unseen data belong in a pretrained model's context or its weights, built on an exact law for their information value. That value, the code length saved on held-out queries, equals in expectation the bits stored about the query, plus a prior repair, minus a decoding loss, for any model, architecture, dataset and task. The law's ceilings certify the choice from one measurement, and an anytime-valid test makes it from held-out queries, with error controlled whenever it stops. Every certificate the ceilings issue on a trained transformer, TabPFN, an occupancy network and a stereo network is correct. Title: Residual-Based Causal Discovery via Pretrained In-Context Quantile Regression
Daniel Klippert, Quang Nhat Huynh, Alexander Marx
Subjects: Machine Learning (stat.ML); Machine Learning (cs.LG)

Residual-based causal discovery assumes that each variable is generated from its parents and an independent noise source. It estimates this noise and tests its independence from candidate causes. For identifiable functions such as additive-noise models (ANMs) and the broader class of location-scale noise models (LSNMs), this principle identifies the causal direction, as only the causal model admits this independence. Existing LSNM methods rely on parametric assumptions to obtain standardized residuals, risking model misspecification. In contrast, the RESIT-framework allows for nonparametric estimation of residuals to iteratively identify sink nodes, but was proposed for ANMs. To close this gap, we propose Loscar, an extension of RESIT to LSNMs that estimates the conditional mean and scale via quantile regression, enabling pretrained tabular foundation models as plug-in estimators. We compare it with a feasible generalized least squares (FGLS) variant, which is consistent given suitable basis functions. Across bivariate and multivariate causal-discovery benchmarks, in-context quantile regression substantially outperforms FGLS with flexible function classes, particularly for multivariate causes.

Title: Near Minimax Rate for Quantile Distributional Reinforcement Learning
Zijie Cheng, Zhihua Zhang
Subjects: Machine Learning (stat.ML); Machine Learning (cs.LG)

In this paper, we study quantile distributional policy evaluation, where the return distribution under a given policy is approximated by the quantile Bellman fixed point $\bm\eta_m$. We investigate the statistical accuracy of estimating $\bm\eta_m$ across quantile resolutions under Wasserstein distances. Assuming access to a generative model, we construct a quantile fixed point estimator $\bm\eta_m^{(n)}$ from an empirical Markov decision process and establish non-asymptotic error bounds under Wasserstein distances. When reward densities are uniformly bounded away from zero, we obtain a root-$n$ high-probability bound simultaneously over all resolutions $m$ and all Wasserstein orders $1\le p\le\infty$. When reward densities vanish at the support endpoints, we show that continuity and endpoint monotonicity suffice to preserve the same resolution-independent rate for $1\le p\le2$. For $p>2$, we establish a root-$n$ bound with coefficient $m^{1/2-1/p}$, together with a slower, resolution-independent $n^{-1/p}$ bound for every finite $p>2$. We further obtain resolution-independent $W_\infty$ consistency governed by the continuity modulus of reward quantile functions.
To investigate the sharpness of these bounds, we characterize the exact local asymptotic minimax risk at each fixed resolution as the expected Wasserstein-coordinate norm of the efficient Gaussian limit and prove that our estimator attains it. We then examine the resolution dependence of this optimal local risk. A regular one-state instance has resolution-independent local risk constants, whereas a single endpoint-vanishing two-state instance exhibits growth of order $m^{1/2-1/p}$, up to logarithmic factors along a resolution subsequence, for every $p>2$.

Jérémie Bigot, Clément Bonet
Subjects: Machine Learning (stat.ML); Machine Learning (cs.LG); Methodology (stat.ME)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a fundamental tool for statistical dimension reduction, yet no canonical extension to distribution-valued data has emerged within the framework of optimal transport. While Wasserstein barycenters and Wasserstein least-squares regression provide canonical liftings of Euclidean averaging and linear regression in the Wasserstein space, an analogous principle for PCA has remained unexplored. In this paper, we introduce Lifted PCA, a canonical extension of Euclidean PCA to Wasserstein space, obtained through the framework of lifting functionals in the space of probability measures by convex extension. Numerical experiments on distributional datasets illustrate the practical performance of the proposed methodology and its benefits for distributional data analysis.

Title: Silent Alignment in Two-Layer Linear Networks: A High-Dimensional Perspective
Simon Martin (DI-ENS, LPENS, SIERRA), Francis Bach (DI-ENS, SIERRA), Giulio Biroli (LPENS)
Subjects: Machine Learning (stat.ML); Machine Learning (cs.LG)

We study gradient flow in a two-layer linear network in the high-dimensional regime where the input, inner and output dimensions diverge proportionally. Under whitened inputs and isotropic imbalance, we combine a closed-form solution of the dynamics with prior random matrix theory results to derive a high-dimensional limit for the dynamics. Our method yields exact asymptotic spectral distributions for the weight matrices at all times and characterizes the evolution of the learned subspace. In the small-initialization limit, we identify a silent alignment phenomenon: the singular directions of the network align on an order-one timescale, while the singular values and empirical loss remain at their initial values. Title: The Price of Current-Effect Inference in Nonstationary Multi-Armed Bandits
Sharan Sahu, Martin T. Wells
Comments: 49 pages, 8 figures, 5 tables
Subjects: Machine Learning (stat.ML); Machine Learning (cs.LG)

Adaptive experiments use bandit algorithms to favor interventions with higher expected rewards. However, decision-makers also require reliable inference on current treatment effects: differences between intervention and reference-arm mean rewards at the present time, rather than historical averages. These objectives conflict: reward maximization favors better interventions, whereas current-effect inference requires continued exploration of potentially suboptimal interventions as outcomes change over time. We characterize this tradeoff for $K$-arm bandits with Hölder-smooth mean rewards of exponent $\beta\in(0,1]$ and constant $L$. Requiring high-probability simultaneous coverage of all current effects by confidence intervals of width at most $w$, we establish matching minimax bounds, up to logarithmic factors, on expected dynamic regret. Over horizon $T$, our algorithm TRACE (Tracking Rewards and Current Effects) achieves regret of order $\widetilde{O}_{\beta}\left(\sqrt{KT}+K^{\beta/(2\beta+1)}L^{1/(2\beta+1)}T^{(\beta+1)/(2\beta+1)}+Kw^{-2}(1+L^{1/\beta}w^{-1/\beta})\right)$, where the notation hides logarithms and constants depending only on $\beta$. This regret--precision tradeoff separates reward-learning regret from the cost of refreshing current-effect estimates, which remains unavoidable even when one arm is uniformly optimal. An empirical-Bernstein e-process extension adapts exploration to realized prediction errors while preserving worst-case guarantees. When within-block drift is negligible relative to sampling error, all observations, including reward-learning observations, can be reused for asymptotically normal pointwise inference and stronger local stationarity yields local asymptotic efficiency. Finally, we show that regret constraints can prevent honest adaptation to unknown smoothness and support our theory with extensive numerical experiments.

We also establish a uniform convergence result for the posterior predictive mean under stated regularity conditions and construct local equivalent weights by matching the first-order sensitivity of a plug-in poststratified estimand to that of a weighted average of fitted response means, with both derivatives taken with respect to the latent mean-scale vector. Simulations show that HGP achieves lower population-weighted prediction error under nonparametric structure, whereas logistic regression performs well under correct parametric specification. Additional analyses examine sample size, frame coverage, subgroup prediction, and population-mean agreement. An application to the Future of Families and Child Wellbeing Study illustrates the method. Overall, this work extends local equivalent-weight construction to nonparametric modeling and provides a computational framework and conditional theoretical results for survey weighting in structured, sparse populations.

Title: Privacy Accounting for Empirical Denoisers
William Cheng, Dongjun Kim, Mijung Park
Comments: 31 pages, 7 figures
Subjects: Machine Learning (cs.LG); Cryptography and Security (cs.CR); Machine Learning (stat.ML)

Diffusion models have emerged as the foundation of modern generative systems, yet their high memorization capacity raises privacy concerns. While differentially private (DP) training provides formal guarantees, it comes at a cost in sample quality. In this work, we study a complementary question: how much privacy loss is incurred during the sampling process itself? Directly analyzing modern neural denoisers is challenging because their sensitivity to a single training example is generally intractable. To overcome this challenge, we construct and analyze a clipped privacy-accountable empirical denoiser, a training-free estimator of the Bayes-optimal denoiser, whose per-step sensitivity can be computed explicitly, allowing each denoising step to be a Gaussian mechanism. This perspective enables privacy accounting under Gaussian DP when each training example contributes to each denoising step, and under $f$-DP trade-off functions when each generated sample uses a single randomly subsampled block of the training data. We report every $(\varepsilon, \delta)$ guarantee as a certified upper bound from a privacy loss distribution accountant, and use a cumulant-based Edgeworth approximation as a fast diagnostic, characterizing where it is reliable and where it becomes anti-conservative. We also analyze the privacy loss along the denoising trajectory and show that it grows steeply near the critical window in which semantic content is formed. This observation motivates hybrid samplers that use the private empirical denoiser through the formative steps where it remains a faithful proxy for the neural denoiser, then switch to a public neural denoiser for the remaining refinement. Our results on CIFAR-10 and CelebA-64 show that the privacy of diffusion models depends not only on the training procedure, but also on sampling-time design choices such as subsampling, stopping time, and hybrid denoising.
