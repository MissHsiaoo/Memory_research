Title: Forcing a Letter Suppresses Refusal: Mechanistic Evidence from WMDP
Stepan Epifantsev, Anna Antipova, Oleg Chernikov, Leon Strelkov, Alexey Dontsov
Comments: Accepted to BlackboxNLP 2026 (EMNLP 2026 workshop). 17 pages, 15 figures
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL); Machine Learning (cs.LG)

Safety benchmarks for large language models (LLMs) commonly use multiple-choice question (MCQ) format. However, prior studies documented that MCQ format reduces refusal rates, which may significantly affect the safety behavior of the model. We provide a first mechanistic explanation of this effect on the Weapons of Mass Destruction Proxy (WMDP) dataset across two models - Llama-3.1-8B and Qwen3-8B. First, we use the tuned lens technique to show that when processing MCQ the model commits to an answer-letter token very quickly, which is in contrast to the gradual refusal build-up in open-ended formats. Additionally, we observe that activations on the MCQ format lie roughly 3x further from the refusal direction, compared with their open-ended counterparts. Moreover, ablating the attention heads that carry the MCQ format answer increases the refusal, which is a direct evidence that the MCQ answer-selection competes with refusal. These findings indicate that MCQ safety scores primarily measure knowledge capabilities and do not assess the safety-mechanisms.

Title: FinEvolveKG: Controlled and Attributable Prompt Evolution for Financial Knowledge Graph Construction
Abhinav Arun, Jayita Bhattacharyya, Fabrizio Dimino, Bhaskarjit Sarmah, Stefano Pasquali
Comments: 8 pages, 1 figure, 5 tables
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL); Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI)

Financial institutions construct knowledge graphs (KGs) from shared SEC 10-Ks to serve risk analysis, portfolio management, and investment research, whose analytical needs differ. Extraction must adapt without fragmenting shared grounding, typing, and temporal rules. Hand-authored persona prompts and fine-tuning add cost. Weight-free prompt optimization offers an alternative, but unconstrained evolution of a monolithic prompt can rewrite shared rules alongside persona guidance, obscuring changes and complicating review. We introduce FinEvolveKG, a KG-construction harness separating reusable general and persona-policy blocks and mechanically confining GEPA, a reflective prompt optimizer, to the designated block. During persona adaptation, the approved general block remains byte-stable and every optimizer edit is localized to a separately versioned persona policy. A dual objective balances generic temporal-KG quality with persona-relevant facts, motifs, and multi-hop connectivity. We evaluate five mutation scopes on ticker-disjoint 10-K splits, with three seeds for the persona-conditioned arms (v3-v5) across three personas. On the three-seed mean, the complete two-stage block-constrained workflow (v4) improves over the frozen prompt baseline (v1) by 19.4-30.9% and over the tested unconstrained full-prompt workflow (v5) by 2.2-6.4%, leading v5 on both objective components and winning all nine paired-seed comparisons with the lowest seed variance in every persona. Without further optimization, v4 also exceeds v5 across all evaluated out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts. An SME audit supports using the generated triplets as ground truth; a blinded two-SME audit favors v4 over v5 in 70.1% of contrast-enriched judgments. These results show that mechanically attributable evolution can coexist with strong empirical performance, without attributing the gains causally to the boundary.

Title: Compression of Emotion in Language Model Representations
Laya Iyer, Sanmi Koyejo, Chen Shani
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL); Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI)

Large language models (LLMs) have been evaluated on their ability to identify emotions expressed in text and produce fluent emotional language. However, output predictions alone do not reveal how fine-grained emotion distinctions are organized internally. We study seven LLMs on GoEmotions, an English Reddit dataset annotated with 27 fine-grained emotion categories plus neutral. We compare model predictions and emotion-prompted final-token representations with the 28 GoEmotions categories and with coarser emotion-family and sentiment groupings.
We find a consistent pattern of $\textit{overcompression}$ relative to the fine-grained GoEmotions taxonomy. Under the tested KMeans protocol, when we cluster their hidden representations without using emotion labels, the clearest split is into two groups that largely separate positive from negative emotion. Coarser emotion groupings are also recovered with fewer clusters than the full 28-category taxonomy. Yet linear probes retain substantial fine-grained predictive information. These results suggest that fine-grained emotion information remains accessible even though broader affective structure more strongly organizes the unsupervised geometry.

Title: Contextuality and Contradictions: Exploring Real-World Identifications of AI-Generated Writing
Michelle Morgenstern, Julia Witte Zimmerman, Tabia Tanzin Prama, Kristine Harootunian, June Clark, Bryn Loftness, Calla Beauregard, Juniper Lovato
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

As AI-generated content spreads into nearly every domain of human interaction, practices and discourses of AI detection, both formal and informal, have become an everyday part of how people interact with text and with each other. In this paper, we explore the real-world practices and discourses of AI identification that have emerged in response to a shifting discursive landscape where the human provenance of a text can no longer be taken for granted. We pair qualitative coding with computational analysis of lexical change, grounded in linguistic anthropological perspectives on language and communication, to investigate AI identifications among English Wikipedia editors and students. We find that these identifications draw on a diversity of evidentiary types, from discrete textual features and judgments about content to circular sense-making claims and presumptions about the author's moral character, and that they are contradictory, dynamic, and contextually dependent. In practice, what often motivates an AI identification is not the presence of any specific textual element, but pragmatic infelicity: a perceived mismatch between the elements present and those deemed appropriate within the text's particular context. Consequently, real-world identifications of AI-generated writing do not merely neutrally point out the perceived presence of "AI-like" features, but can be leveraged as a discursive resource to maintain and define sociolinguistic norms: what can be said in a given context, what is appropriate, and, ultimately, what is human. Further, the design and implementation of systems and tools that treat AI-likeness as a context-free property of text may therefore inherit, and enforce at scale, the norms of whichever contexts they were trained on.

Title: RL-ATTACK: A Generalized Black-Box Adversarial Attack on Language Models via Reinforcement Learning
Taikun Chen, Yue Zhang, Wei Qian, Hui Fang
Comments: Accepted as a regular paper at IEEE EdgeCom 2026
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL); Cryptography and Security (cs.CR)

Large language models (LLMs) exhibit vulnerabilities to adversarial perturbations that can induce unintended responses. These vulnerabilities can be identified via intentional attacks and subsequently used to improve model safety. In black-box settings where gradients are inaccessible, existing methods tend to guess adversarial transformations using external knowledge, ignoring the information provided by the target model. To mitigate this, we propose a trainable black-box framework based on reinforcement learning, which learns from the target model to generate tailored adversarial transformations. We demonstrate that our approach achieves lower post-attack accuracy, greater query efficiency and better semantic preservation on both natural language inference and generative Q&A tasks. On natural language inference, it drives post-attack accuracy to 2% within 20 queries. Against generative language models, our trained attacker consistently achieves lower post-attack accuracy than the untrained attacker and wins in 4 out of 6 settings when compared to a strong optimization-based baseline. We further experiment with multiple reward designs and training settings, and we find that no design or setting is consistently dominant. Our code is available at this https URL.

Title: Structuring Survey Histories for LLM Digital Twins: A Controlled Study of Background, Decision Procedure, and Evaluation Profiles
Iris Ye, Tianze Deng, Ozan Candogan
Comments: 10 pages. Camera-ready version for SocialAgent: Second Workshop on Large Language Models for Social Reasoning and Simulation, NeurIPS 2026
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

LLM-based digital twins aim to predict how individuals would respond in new settings from representations of their prior responses. Existing evidence that compression of survey histories need not reduce predictive accuracy suggests that representation structure, rather than information volume alone, may be an important design choice. We study BDE, a fixed representation that organizes each respondent's survey history into Background, Decision procedure, and Evaluation. We evaluate it on Twin-2K-500 (Toubia et al., 2025) by predicting each respondent's held-out answers on 17 tasks. In a paired evaluation with 100 respondents, a fully inferred BDE profile improves overall accuracy over the raw survey transcript by 1.27 percentage points (pp) on gpt-5.4-nano (p < 0.01) and 1.19 pp on gpt-5.4-mini (p < 0.001). Three analyses then examine the schema, extraction depth, and component composition. Against a length-matched free-form profile written from the same transcript by the same extractor, BDE improves pricing accuracy on both simulators (2.15 pp on nano, p < 0.01; 1.38 pp on mini, p < 0.05), while its overall advantage is significant only on nano (1.05 pp, p < 0.05). Allowing deeper inference during extraction yields no statistically significant gain on either simulator. By contrast, adding Evaluation to a Background--Decision procedure profile improves overall accuracy by 1.44 pp on nano (p < 0.001) and 1.22 pp on mini (p < 0.001). These results indicate that the advantage over raw transcripts is not explained by compression or length alone, and attribute it primarily to profile composition rather than deeper extraction.

Title: Semantic Conditional Similarity (SCS): A Formal Mathematical Framework
Mehmet Murat Albayrakoğlu
Comments: 34 pages, 6 figures, 8 tables, to be submitted for publication
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

Semantic Textual Similarity (STS) operates on document pairs, returning a single scalar per comparison. In a multi-document setting where several sources may have contributed to one or more targets, pairwise STS cannot disentangle each source's marginal semantic contribution from their shared vocabulary. In this paper, Semantic Conditional Similarity (SCS) is introduced as a conditional form of STS that computes similarity between two documents after eliminating semantic elements shared with a third via set-theoretic operations. SCS makes four contributions: 1) it introduces directionality, partitioning pairwise relations into lateral (inter-source) and vertical (source-to-target) classes; 2) conditioning induces role-dependent asymmetry by extending the comparison domain from unordered pairs to ordered, conditioned triples of a target, a reference, and a conditioning document; 3) SCS introduces inter-document conditioning by subtracting shared content from a third document, complementing the intra-document conditioning in recent C-STS work; and 4) the set-theoretic decomposition achieves threshold-free partitioning of shared and residual content, requiring no arbitrary similarity cutoff. Although developed for the $n \to 1$ configuration, the framework extends to $1 \to n$ via vertical role reversal and to $m \to n$ via independent composition of source- and target-side conditioning. With the established $1 \to 1$ case, these span the full spectrum of document cardinality. SCS scores should be interpreted ordinally, as rankings of marginal contributions rather than absolute measurements. The broader implication is that metric space axioms commonly assumed in similarity formalizations constrain expressiveness in role-differentiated multi-document settings, and that extending the domain to conditioned triples can recover information that pairwise-symmetric comparisons suppress.

Title: Guardrail Erosion in Domain Fine-Tuned Large Language Models: A Paired Audit of Knowledge-Centric Abstention in Biomedical LLMs
Stephen Peng, Vineet Jagadeesan Nair, Noah Broestl, Anne Friederike Kleppe, Steven Mills, Adi Zolotov
Comments: To be published in the Proceedings of the 6th Machine Learning for Health Symposium (ML4H 2026), PMLR volume 346
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL); Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI)

The number of domain fine-tuned models, particularly in the biomedical domain, is rapidly expanding. Fine-tuning promises high-performing models at a fraction of the cost of training a base model, but it can also produce unintended behavioral changes. Multiple studies have shown that safety guardrails can erode with fine-tuning, yet large-scale studies across model architectures, data mixtures, and fine-tuning methodologies remain limited. Where prior work in this space has studied value-centric refusal, this work focuses on knowledge-centric abstention. We audit 25 biomedical domain fine-tuned large language models against 11 paired base checkpoints on MedAbstain and ClinDet-Bench to evaluate the impact of fine-tuning on knowledge-centric abstention. We find that defined appropriate abstention measured by these benchmarks, already low in base models (median 8.3% on MedAbstain), falls further after fine-tuning (median 4.2%, decreasing in 21 of 25 pairs on MedAbstain), and that the magnitude and direction of change are heterogeneous across pairs. These results motivate fine-tuning methods that improve domain-specific capability while preserving appropriate caution.

Title: Richness Is Not Evenness: Ordered Renyi Crossings under Self-Training
Pranjal Srivastava
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

Recursive self-training of language models, a setting studied in model collapse, can reduce vocabulary while making frequent words, mostly function words, less dominant. We fine-tune English GPT-2 on its own output for nine generations, vary the training-data sampling temperature, and measure every model with one fixed sampler. As training temperature rises, Renyi entropy changes turn from loss to gain at distinct temperatures: min-entropy first, then collision entropy, Shannon entropy and observed richness. Between the outer crossings, word types fall while the most-common word becomes less dominant. Untruncated training at T = 1 lies in this interval: at 13,000 words, generation 9 has 24% fewer types than generation 0 yet collision entropy is 0.91 bits higher. The ordering persists with a finer grid, different measurement samplers, a fixed word budget and five fresh training seeds. Min-entropy again crosses before richness in four further settings (in Hindi only at interpolated roots) and in 12 of 13 distinct measurements under eight text representations. Next-token identities and a categorical simulation isolate decoding and sampling effects; the crossing order remains empirical. Fine-tuning on human text can produce the same split while likelihood improves, and no single order's sign reliably identifies worse likelihood across the tested settings. We recommend reporting entropy profiles at stated budgets with an independent quality measure.

Wenkai Yu, Ning Ding, Wentao Zhang, Yehui Tang
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

As language agents tackle increasingly complex tasks, they increasingly rely on multiple agents to coordinate specialized work, requiring decisions not only about what each subagent should do but also which tools and workspace resources it should access. Existing agent systems provide mechanisms for controlling tools and execution environments, but do not directly specify the resources required by each delegated subtask. Moreover, task outcomes and execution trajectories alone provide limited supervision for determining whether a subagent has received appropriate permissions. We observe that Agent Skills encode procedural knowledge describing the operations, tools, and files required at each step, providing a natural source of supervision for authorization. These results show that the procedural knowledge embedded in Agent Skills provides a source of supervision for precise subagent orchestration that task outcomes alone cannot provide.

Title: On the Incompleteness of do-calculus for Universal Identifiability
Yaroslav Kivva, Aliaksandra Novik, Negar Kiyavash
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL); Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI); Machine Learning (cs.LG); Probability (math.PR)

We study the problem of causal effect identification from an arbitrary set of observational and/or interventional distributions, which we call Universal Identifiability (uID). The uID problem generalizes existing classical identifiability (ID) and general identifiability (gID) problems, which considered the identification problem from either mere observational distributions or a specific type of interventional distributions. Prior research has established graphical criteria to determine when a causal effect is identifiable in ID and gID problems. Moreover, algorithms that leverage do-calculus and basic probability manipulations have been proposed to compute these effects. Consequently, it has been shown that do-calculus is both sound and complete in these problems. Despite these well-established results, a formal definition of what it means for the do-calculus to be complete does not exist. We propose a rigorous formalization for the completeness of the do-calculus and show that the completeness results in previous work remain valid. However, for more general problems, such as uID, the rules of do-calculus are no longer complete. This is perhaps the reason why the uID problem has remained open thus far.

Title: Beyond Truncation? Entailment-Licensed Elision and the Limits of Semantic Preservation in LLM Decoding
Mahmoud Alyosify, Mirna Imbabi, Eman Elkhamisi
Comments: 8 pages, 3 figures, 2 tables
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

Instruction-tuned large language models (LLMs) answer at greater length than a request needs. We ask whether a training-free controller inside the decoding loop can remove that surplus while preserving content, judged against the simplest possible baseline: truncating the model's own answer to the same length. An entropy-gated logits processor removes 25.5-34.9% of tokens on MT-Bench development questions and 19.9-34.2% on a sealed test split within pre-registered BERTScore non-inferiority margins, yet we prove and confirm by audit that its output is always a prefix of the unmodified answer: the saving is truncation. ELIDE beats matched-length truncation on three model families, but reading 936 of its elisions on 670 public prompts shows that 59% harm the answer, a failure that no automatic measure detects. Scoping the licence to its discourse unit reduces the harmful share to about one elision in ten on two fresh tests.

Title: Failure Profiling and Reachable Trajectory Selection for Reasoning Distillation
Yijun Zhu, Jianxin Wang, Chengchao Shen
Comments: Accepted to NeurIPS 2026. The source code and trained weights are available at \url{this https URL}
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL); Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI); Machine Learning (cs.LG)

Training smaller language models on reasoning solutions generated by a larger teacher model is the prevailing approach for transferring mathematical reasoning capabilities. However, the student tends to overfit the output distribution of the teacher and generalizes poorly when its own behavior at inference time deviates from the training data. Recent methods attempt to bridge this gap by incorporating error data, yet the corrective trajectories still reflect the reasoning patterns of the teacher, which the student cannot reliably reproduce, leaving the distribution mismatch largely unresolved. We propose a method, Failure Profiling and Reachable Trajectory Selection (FPRT), built on the insight that effective training data should be jointly determined by what the student fails on and what it can realistically learn. Our method first profiles the failure behavior of the student on each problem through repeated sampling, characterizing it in terms of error rate and error consistency to reveal qualitatively distinct failure modes. Each mode demands a fundamentally different training signal. The diagnosed mode then guides candidate generation from the teacher via distinct prompting strategies tailored to each failure type. Among the correct candidates, our method selects the one whose initial reasoning steps after the point of divergence are most reproducible by the student. These first corrective steps constitute the primary bottleneck, while the remaining steps follow an already corrected context and can therefore be continued more readily. This contrasts with standard reranking approaches, which score the trajectory as a whole and thereby dilute the signal at the critical reasoning transition. Experiments demonstrate that our method achieves the SOTA performance across the five benchmarks on both student backbones.

Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

We compared DeepSeek-R1 vs. DeepSeek-V3, our closest available contrast for reasoning-oriented post-training, and GPT-5 vs. GPT-4o, through 60 blinded pairwise evaluations in which seven human annotators and an ensemble LLM-as-a-Judge rated prompt-matched story outputs. These findings suggest that, at the frontier, reasoning-related quality differences are small enough to be dominated by reader subjectivity, and that LLM-as-a-Judge evaluators can obscure this subjectivity in nuanced creative-generation tasks.
