Title: Discourse Analysis of Legislative Bill Discussions
Isaac Rudnick, Nipun Das, Foaad Khosmood
Comments: 10 pages, 4 figures
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

Legislative hearings are distinct communication events with their own human recognizable structure and logic, yet they have been analyzed as distinct phenomena beyond their transcripts. Understanding legislative hearings at a deeper level is a major step in analyzing the work and power relationships within democratic bodies. In this work, we present a novel end-to-end pipeline for parsing rhetorical structures from legislative committee hearings with the goal of improving legislative discourse analysis and democratic accountability. We also propose a rhetorical structure grammar and find that 55.21% of California state bill discussions in our dataset can be parsed with this grammar. We evaluate our pipeline with two utterance section classifiers on a random subset of over 2,000 annotated utterances corresponding to 100 hearings. We find that a logistic regression-based classifier with grammar-based output masking and utterance tag smoothing outperforms more complex models, achieving an F1 score of 52.8% against our ground truth discourse section labels.

Title: CCDWIKI: Decoupling Case Formulation from Response Generation in Longitudinal CBT Dialogue
Mingwen Zhang, Ruiyang Song, Qinying Wang, Yuchen Wang, Changsheng Ma, Minqiang Yang
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) demands more than empathic validation, requiring instead a structured formulation of the interplay among situations, automatic thoughts, emotions, and behaviors. Therapists formalize this interplay through a Cognitive Conceptualization Diagram, where maintaining longitudinal coherence necessitates continuous evidential adjudication to separate tentative hypotheses from established findings. Current dialogue systems track conversational history via retrieval or static profiles but lack the representational capacity to encode epistemic status. Consequently, provisional interpretations are often prematurely reified as established facts, inadvertently biasing downstream interventions. Addressing this limitation, CCDWiki is introduced as a plug-and-play advisor conceptualizing longitudinal therapy as evidence-grounded hypothesis adjudication. The approach encodes each clinical hypothesis as a mutable entry bound to corroborating and contradicting evidence. Across sessions, accumulating data strengthens supported entries, preserves provisional ones, and retracts contradicted assumptions. The resulting formulation distills into concise guidance for unmodified therapeutic language models. Experiments demonstrate substantial improvements in both conceptualization and intervention quality without requiring backbone retraining.

Title: Human-like cross-language generalisation in deep neural speaker embeddings and its acoustic foundations
Tianze Xu, Xiyang Li, Xiaoming Jiang, Volker Dellwo
Comments: This manuscript has been accepted for presentation at Interspeech 2026. Code is available at this https URL. The archived release is available on Zenodo at this https URL
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL); Sound (cs.SD); Audio and Speech Processing (eess.AS)

Generalisability is a central question for deep neural networks, including the cross-language generalisation of speaker embeddings. This study examined whether deep neural speaker embeddings generalise across languages and how such generalisation relates to human perception and acoustic structure. Using Cantonese--English bilingual speech presented in within- and cross-language conditions, we derived similarity from 18 state-of-the-art models and compared it with human ratings and acoustic features using linear mixed models and representational similarity analysis. Embeddings exhibited cross-language generalisation that broadly paralleled human behaviour, reflecting accurate identity discrimination and sensitivity to language switching. This generalisation relied on largely shared acoustic cues with language-sensitive weighting, mirroring human patterns. Together, these findings provide converging evidence for human--machine alignment in cross-language voice similarity processing.

Title: Same Origin, Different Verdicts: Text Production Conditions Confound Machine-Generated Text Detection in Russian
Stanislav Kirichenko
Comments: 65 pages, 6 figures, 24 tables. English translation of the frozen Russian manuscript; the Russian text is the source of record and is available with all data, code and run manifests at this https URL (v1.0.1)
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

Automated detection of machine-generated text is applied where the verdict carries a cost for the author, yet origin is a property of the production process, not an observable property of the text. The conditions under which a text is produced push it in the same direction in which a generative model is presumed to push it. This study measures the contribution of these conditions while origin is held fixed. The Russian-language corpus of 1882 documents comprises a machine part, generated by four generation channels (each backed by its own model family) across 45 tasks in three task modes, and an archival human part annotated for source regimentation level before the texts were read. The primary contrast is computed within the machine corpus: both halves of a pair are generated by the same model for the same task and differ only in task wording. Machine-likeness is measured by four operationalisations: an interpretable index with weights fixed before data collection, a trained classifier, a zero-shot metric on an open model, a judge model. The main result is disagreement between procedures: changing the task mode shifted the scores of all four, but three gave a negative shift, the judge a positive one, and none of the four intervals covers zero. The comparison plan and the specifications of procedures 2-4 were fixed after the result of procedure 1, before procedures 2-4 ran; the disagreement survived correction of a corpus defect and of the nested validation. A baseline on the source identifier alone reaches a median AUROC of 1.0000 on the thirteen group splits where it is defined, and removing emphasis markers flipped a confident machine verdict to human without changing the full text by a single character. The scores are properties of a specific measurement system and corpus: they reflect data collection, repetition, formatting, and preprocessing decisions as well as linguistic style.

Title: SRRM: A Retrieval-Reconstruction Benchmark for Evaluating Long-Context Memory in Small Language Models
Christopher Alberto Hamonangan
Comments: 37 pages, 20 tables, 13 figures
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

Small Language Models (SLMs) are increasingly deployed on consumer-grade hardware, yet their ability to preserve information across progressively longer contexts remains insufficiently understood. Existing benchmarks primarily evaluate retrieval-oriented tasks, providing limited insight into whether successful retrieval reflects comprehensive long-context memory preservation. To address this limitation, this paper introduces Simple Retrieval-Reconstruction Memory (SRRM), a lightweight and fully reproducible benchmark that jointly evaluates Targeted Retrieval and Global Information Reconstruction. This study also proposes the Memory Preservation Ratio (MPR), a benchmark-level metric that quantifies the relationship between retrieval and reconstruction performance. SRRM was evaluated using a synthetic dataset on three Qwen2.5 models (0.5B, 1.5B, and 3B parameters) across context loads ranging from 10 to 100 factual statements. Experimental results show that reconstruction performance deteriorated substantially faster than retrieval accuracy as contextual information increased, indicating that retrieval alone is insufficient to characterize long-context memory preservation. Among the evaluated models, Qwen2.5-1.5B achieved the highest retrieval accuracy (86.7%), reconstruction performance (45.7%), and MPR (0.53), outperforming the larger Qwen2.5-3B model (72.2%, 18.5%, and 0.26, respectively). These findings demonstrate that increasing parameter count does not necessarily improve long-context memory preservation. Overall, SRRM provides a reproducible and extensible benchmark for systematically evaluating long-context memory while revealing complementary memory behaviors that remain hidden under retrieval-only evaluation frameworks.

The rewrite is character-aligned to the OCR input, its differences are split into blocks, and a 0.6B-parameter gate keeps or reverts each block. Title: RAGCompass: Generalizable Retriever Routing via Reranking
Valentin Six, Mufei Li, Dongqi Fu, Yinglong Xia, Hong Li, Pan Li
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL); Information Retrieval (cs.IR)

Different retrievers exist for retrieval-augmented generation, yet none performs best for all queries. Therefore, the key question is: given a query and retrieved passages from different retrievers, which one is most suited for that query? Existing routers usually make predictions from query-specific features and a fixed pool of retrievers; such routers have no output for an unseen retriever and are incapable by design on a new corpus. To fill these gaps, RAGCompass reads the retrieved passage sets themselves: passages from an unfamiliar retriever or corpus stay directly comparable to other passages, so routing stays well designed where a query-only map would fail. We contrastively fine-tune a reranker against the answer each evidence set produces, which accounts for most of the gain; a small comparison head adds a moderate improvement, and neither component has retriever-specific parameters. RAGCompass improves answer F1 by 28% relative to the best fixed retriever, and outperforms fusion baselines, three query-only routers, and a prompted 8B language model. Generalization experiments show that RAGCompass keeps most of its gain under an unseen corpus, retriever, or reader, while query-only baselines fall to the best fixed retriever or below.

Title: Cognitive Bias Signatures in Search-Equipped LLM Agents: A Stage-Resolved Analysis
Yanru Xiao, Yingwei Yu
Comments: 19 pages, 4 figures
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

Search can help LLM agents correct mistakes, but their starting conditions may shape which evidence they find and use. Drawing on cognitive-bias paradigms, we develop a controlled search harness to examine result order, records of prior work, and initial hypotheses while holding each question and document collection fixed. Matched controls distinguish order effects from differences in document composition and the effects of failed work from context length. Across 10 models and 1,000 synthetic search worlds, we track queries, retrieved documents, document openings, and final answers. Our comparisons suggest that final answers are more sensitive to initial evidence order during active search than when all evidence is supplied in full. Exploratory interventions show that opening counter-evidence more often can coincide with lower accuracy, while accuracy can improve with little change in opening.

Title: Prospective Memory for Proactive LLM Agents
Yuanchen Bei, Zhining Liu, Tianxin Wei, Weizhi Zhang, Zhichen Zeng, Philip S. Yu, Hendrik Hamann, Jingrui He, Hanghang Tong
Comments: 17 pages, 8 figures
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

Most memory systems for LLM agents are retrospective, preserving and retrieving past information to inform current decisions. Proactive agents, however, also require prospective memory: retaining future-directed intentions and realizing them when appropriate opportunities arise, even without explicit requests. Crucially, remembering the right intention does not guarantee acting at the right time: a related observation may call for waiting, while a later cue may make the same intention actionable. Inspired by cognitive theories of human prospective memory, we introduce Pro-Memory, a memory framework that separates maintaining future intentions from deciding when and how to realize them. Pro-Memory preserves pending objectives linked to their supporting evidence, assesses new observations against their original goals and activation conditions, and coordinates appropriate contributions with the agent's ongoing actions. It further updates intention states based on actions actually issued or evidence of resolution, while an experience memory distills feedback from completed episodes into guidance for future decisions. Experiments on two benchmarks spanning delayed-intention tasks and open-ended proactive assistance demonstrate consistent improvements in proactive task performance over representative memory approaches across multiple LLM backbones, while limiting unnecessary interventions.

Title: How Well Can Large Language Models Impersonate Individual Authors? A Comparative Evaluation
Harshit Gupta, Shunichi Ishihara
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL); Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI)

This study investigates the authorship impersonation capacity of six widely used large language models (LLMs): Gemini 3 Pro, Claude Opus, GPT 5.2, GPT 5.2 Pro, DeepSeek 3.2, and Grok 4.1 Fast. The evaluation was conducted using short product review texts selected to ensure high within author consistency in individual writing style. Two types of in context prompt were designed: a simple prompt with minimal instruction and a complex prompt incorporating explicit stylometric guidance for mimicry. Authorship mimicry was evaluated using a cosine distance based distance to training metric, supplemented by visual analyses of embedding distributions. The results indicate that authorship impersonation remained challenging for most LLMs, with Gemini 3 Pro showing the strongest apparent mimicry performance. However, subsequent overlap analysis revealed that Gemini 3 Pro extensively copied and reused sentences and phrases from the training texts, at levels far exceeding those observed among human authors. Follow up factor analyses showed that training set consistency was the most robust predictor of mimicry success across models, whereas author distinctiveness did not generalise as a reliable predictor. Global embedding visualisations further revealed a consistent macro level pattern: for most models, generated texts occupied regions of the embedding space that were largely separated from the corresponding human training clusters. This indicates limited convergence towards target author style and suggests the presence of model specific lexical and phraseological preferences. The findings highlight the importance of distinguishing genuine stylistic imitation from direct textual reuse and have implications for the forensic analysis of textual evidence involving potential LLM enabled impersonation.

Title: Phonological Knowledge of Chinese Characters in Language Models
Xiaozhe Ji, Renfen Hu, Hai Hu
Comments: 17 pages, 11 figures, 10 tables
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

How much phonological knowledge do language models (LMs) possess about Chinese characters, and how is this knowledge represented and used? We introduce PinyinBench, a controlled benchmark covering homophones, phonological features (initials, finals, and tones), and polyphonic characters. We investigate 17 text-only and multimodal LMs through three complementary approaches: behavioral evaluation, layer-wise probing, and activation steering. Behaviorally, models demonstrate substantial knowledge of initials and finals, with a frontier model (DS-v4-flash) scoring 90%+ in homophone judgment, when two characters share no phonological features. However, its accuracy drops sharply to 72% when two characters differ only in lexical tone. Probing reveals that tone is highly decodable from embeddings and improves modestly across layers, whereas segmental information starts less decodable and improves substantially with depth. Activation steering shows that segmental representations can systematically influence generation, while tone exhibits much weaker effects. Furthermore, audio-bearing training improves phonological decodability, including tone, without corresponding improvements in steering effectiveness. Together, these findings reveal distinct representational and functional profiles for segmental and tonal information, with tonal information much harder to access and manipulate, demonstrating the necessity for combined evaluation, probing, and causal intervention for understanding linguistic knowledge in LMs.

Expert deactivation at inference time changes both token routing and expert weights, and its effect on performance depends on how these changes are implemented. We compare removal, freezing, remaining-expert renormalization, and unnormalized re-routing on identical disabled sets across five instruction-tuned checkpoints from four model families, with a base OLMoE checkpoint as an additional comparison. We develop a weight-sum model that relates the loss of expert contributions and changes in branch scale to the weight rule used during training. Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL); Artificial Intelligence (cs.AI)

In many applications, humans remain essential for providing private context, authority, or judgments unavailable to the agent. We formulate human decision capacity allocation: given multiple consultation opportunities and a limited budget, at which decisions should an LLM agent consult a human? Title: Familiar letters, not related languages, let unseen languages into a shared space of meaning
Yuliang Liu, Xinyu Wang, Xiang Bai
Subjects: Computation and Language (cs.CL)

Hundreds of languages have written text but almost no translations, and whether a model learning one from untranslated text builds on a related language or familiar letters is unclear: they usually coincide. Here we show that familiar letters, not a related language, let an unseen language into a shared space of meaning. GlyphMind, a tokenizer-free vision transformer that reads text as pixels and lets any sentence be shown in any script, was pretrained to align translations across 27 writing systems. Adapted on untranslated Lao, a language and script it had never seen, it retrieved English translations seven times as often as the strongest simple baseline.
